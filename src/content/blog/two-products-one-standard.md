AI has moved from answering to acting. A model can now issue a refund, enter a bill or open a pull request. The question companies ask has changed with it. It is no longer *"is the model good enough?"* It is **"can we trust it with this?"**

That question is what Quanta is built around.

## Two products

**Factum runs operations.** Its agents work inside the systems a business already runs and do jobs end to end: working a billing ticket, chasing an overdue invoice, turning a vendor invoice into a bill, turning a customer's bug report into an engineering issue. Factum is live.

**Our software foundry builds software.** Its agents take work from requirements to release (design, code, tests and pull requests), with engineers reviewing every change before it merges. It is in development, and the waitlist is open.

Different buyers, different work. The same problem underneath.

## One standard

Every Quanta product follows the same four rules.

1. **Does the work.** The agent reads the real records, follows your own rules and prepares the finished change, not a suggestion for someone else to complete.
2. **Asks first.** Nothing changes until a person says yes. In Factum that's an approval of the exact change; in the software foundry it's a review before merge. Each agent gets only the access its job needs.
3. **Proven first.** Work is checked against real cases before it goes live, so a fix for one thing can't quietly break another.
4. **On record.** Every step is recorded: what started it, what the agent did, who approved it and what it cost.

## Why one standard

> Trust doesn't transfer between rules.

A team that has learned what "approved" and "on record" mean in one product shouldn't have to learn it again in the next. And the hard part of putting agents to work is the same everywhere: knowing what the agent will do, and proving what it did.
